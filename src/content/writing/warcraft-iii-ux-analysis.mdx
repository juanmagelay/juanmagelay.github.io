I lead a UX team that designs messaging products. I'm also studying for a bachelor's degree in [Video Game and Simulation Development](https://unahur.edu.ar/licenciatura-en-desarrollo-de-videojuegos-y-simulaciones/) at Universidad Nacional de Hurlingham, in Buenos Aires, Argentina. To connect both paths, I'm taking [Celia Hodent's Game UX course](https://ixdf.org/courses/game-ux-design-ultimate-guide) at the Interaction Design Foundation (IxDF).

The first assignment asks you to **analyze, as a UX designer, a video game you remember playing a lot**: what made it work, what didn't, and what you can improve in the player experience. This post is my answer.

I chose Warcraft III because it's the game where **everything I liked in the genres of that era came together** for the first time. Comparing it with Age of Empires I and II (AoE), the strategy games I had played the most before, helped me see what Blizzard changed and why it worked.

## Three genres in one game

Before Warcraft III, I had spent a lot of hours on AoE and StarCraft, where what I enjoyed most was planning the moves I needed to win. I also played games with RPG progression, like the Pokémon games (Red, Blue, Gold and Silver), Diablo II and even Mario Tennis on Game Boy Color, and loved making my characters stronger with new abilities and items. And I was a fan of 3D adventures like Super Mario 64, Sonic 3D Blast and Crash Bandicoot.

The first time I played Warcraft III, I realized **it brought those three worlds together in a single game**. The core loop was the one I knew from AoE: gather resources, build a base, train an army and attack. But my army had a hero, who gained experience, leveled up to 10, learned new abilities and carried items. And the world was in 3D: an angled overhead camera by default, and the mouse wheel let me zoom in for a much closer look. Nostalgia probably plays a part here, but I'll try to keep this analysis objective.

## UX analysis

### Usability and engageability

Warcraft III was easy to pick up because **it didn't reinvent the controls**. It followed the RTS conventions I already knew from AoE and StarCraft: the HUD sat at the bottom of the screen with the minimap, unit info and commands, so I could control everything without covering the main scene. Sound did a lot of the feedback work. Unit voices and alerts told me when a building was finished or my base was under attack, even when I was looking somewhere else. That's *usability*: I always knew what was happening and how to act.

But **usability alone doesn't explain why I kept playing**. Matches were about half as long as in AoE (mine took around an hour, while in AoE they could last two). Flying units, which AoE never had, took the action off the ground. And the hero gave me something AoE never did: a character to care about. Losing regular units was part of the plan, but the hero was someone to train and take care of.

### Frustration by design or usability problem?

Two moments made me think about this difference. The first was losing my hero. It hurt: fighting without him was a big disadvantage, I lost the chance to train him in that battle and I paid to revive him at the altar. But he came back with his level and items, so **the game punished my mistake without wiping out my progress**. That's *frustration by design*: a cost placed on purpose so I'd take care of him.

The second was the 12-unit selection limit. Coming from AoE, where I could select much larger groups, it felt like a usability problem: dragging a box over a big army only selected part of it. Later I understood it was intentional. It pushed me to manage smaller groups and make better use of my hero's abilities. **The challenge was by design, but the way I discovered it wasn't.** Nothing on screen explained the limit, so for a while it felt like the game was failing me.

### Genre and platform considerations

RTS players expect fast, readable control, and RPG players expect a sense of progression. Mixing both is risky: too much RPG and the strategy slows down, too little and the hero is just another unit. Warcraft III balanced it by **keeping progression inside the match**: the hero leveled up by fighting, so getting stronger happened while I played the strategy, not instead of it. And the level 10 cap kept the hero from replacing the army.

On platform, Warcraft III was PC only, and Blizzard never released a console version. It makes sense: **an RTS needs the precision of a mouse and keyboard** to select units and give orders quickly, something a gamepad makes much harder.

Argentina added its own layer. 2002 was the year of the economic crisis, and the consoles in most homes were older and cheaper: the original PlayStation, the Family Game (an NES clone), the Sega Genesis or the Nintendo 64. The PlayStation 2, GameCube and Xbox were luxuries. I practiced Warcraft III at home and **played it multiplayer in internet cafés** (cybers, as we called them), and that's where it took off.

## My take as a UX Designer

**Keep:** The hero system and its death penalty. Reviving at the altar with level and items intact **is a fair cost**: strong enough to make you protect your hero, light enough that one mistake doesn't end the match. I'd also keep the sound feedback, which lets players manage a whole base without checking every corner of the map.

**Change:** I don't know what constraints existed back then, in 2002, so let's assume the 12-unit limit had to stay. **The gap I'd flag is how the limit is communicated.** The first time a box selection goes over 12 units, a short hint could point the player to control groups (<kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + a number key). With one message, I think the limit stops feeling like a bug and starts reading as a rule. In September 2026, Blizzard raised the maximum selection from 12 to 24 units in [Warcraft III: Reforged Patch 3.0](https://liquipedia.net/warcraft/Patch_3.0.0), which suggests the friction was real for many players. But raising the limit changes how the game plays, while a hint fixes the communication and leaves the design intact.

## What analyzing the player experience taught me

The clearest lesson from looking at Warcraft III as a designer: **usability and engageability do different jobs**. Familiar controls and good feedback made the game easy to learn, but the hero, the shorter matches and the flying units are what made me want to keep playing. And not every frustration is a problem to fix: the hero's death was a cost worth keeping, while the selection limit only needed better communication.
