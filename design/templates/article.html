When strategy met RPG heroes: a UX look at Warcraft III

A game UX analysis: usability, engageability and frustration by design in Blizzard's 2002 RTS.

I lead a UX team that designs messaging products. I’m also studying for a bachelor’s degree in Video Game and Simulation Development at Universidad Nacional de Hurlingham, in Buenos Aires, Argentina. To connect both paths, I’m taking Celia Hodent’s Game UX course at the Interaction Design Foundation (IxDF).

The first assignment asks you to pick a game moment that stuck with you and analyze it as a UX designer: what made it work, what didn’t, and what it teaches about player experience. This post is my answer.

I chose Warcraft III because it’s the game where everything I liked as a player came together for the first time. Comparing it with Age of Empires (AoE), the strategy game I had played the most before, helped me see what Blizzard changed and why it worked.

The moment

Before Warcraft III, I had spent a lot of hours on Age of Empires I and II and StarCraft, where what I enjoyed most was planning the moves I needed to win. I also played games with RPG progression, like the Pokémon games (Red, Blue, Gold and Silver), Diablo II and even Mario Tennis on Game Boy Color, and loved making my characters stronger with new abilities and items. And I was a fan of 3D adventures like Super Mario 64, Sonic 3D Blast and Crash Bandicoot.

The first time I played Warcraft III, I realized it brought those three worlds together in a single game. The core loop was the one I knew from AoE: gather resources, build a base, train an army and fight. But my army had a hero, who gained experience, leveled up to 10, learned new abilities and carried items. And the world was in 3D: an angled overhead camera by default, and the mouse wheel let me zoom in for a much closer look. I didn’t have to choose between the games I liked anymore. Nostalgia probably plays a part here, but I’ll try to keep this analysis objective.

UX analysis

Usability vs. engageability

Warcraft III was easy to pick up because it didn’t reinvent the controls. It followed the RTS conventions I already knew from AoE and StarCraft: the HUD sat at the bottom of the screen with the minimap, unit info and commands, so I could control everything without covering the main scene. Sound did a lot of the feedback work. Unit voices and alerts told me when a building was finished or my base was under attack, even when I was looking somewhere else. That’s usability: I always knew what was happening and how to act.

But usability alone doesn’t explain why I kept playing. Matches were about half as long as in AoE (mine took around an hour, while in AoE they could last two), so each session felt complete. Flying units, which AoE never had, took the action off the ground. And the hero gave me something AoE never did: a character to care about. Losing regular units was part of the plan. Losing my hero felt personal.

Frustration by design or usability problem?

Two moments made me think about this difference. The first was losing my hero. It hurt: I had to fight without him for a while and pay to revive him at the altar. But he came back with his level and items, so the game punished my mistake without wiping out my progress. That’s frustration by design: a cost placed on purpose so I’d take care of him.

The second was the 12-unit selection limit. Coming from AoE, where I could select much larger groups, it felt like a usability problem: dragging a box over a big army only selected part of it. Later I understood it was intentional. It pushed me to manage smaller groups and make better use of my hero’s abilities. The challenge was by design, but the way I discovered it wasn’t. Nothing on screen explained the limit, so for a while it felt like the game was failing me.

Genre and platform considerations

RTS players expect fast, readable control, and RPG players expect a sense of progression. Mixing both is risky: too much RPG and the strategy slows down, too little and the hero is just another unit. Warcraft III balanced it by keeping progression inside the match: the hero leveled up by fighting, so getting stronger happened while I played the strategy, not instead of it. And the level 10 cap kept the hero from replacing the army.

On platform, Warcraft III was PC only, and Blizzard never released a console version. It makes sense: an RTS needs the precision of a mouse and keyboard to select units and give orders quickly, something a gamepad makes much harder.

Argentina added its own layer. 2002 was the year of the economic crisis, and the consoles in most homes were older and cheaper: the original PlayStation, the Family Game (an NES clone), the Sega Genesis or the Nintendo 64. The PlayStation 2, GameCube and Xbox were luxuries. PC gaming lived in internet cafés, cybers as we called them, and that’s where Warcraft III took off. For me and a lot of players, the platform wasn’t a PC at home but a room full of them, connected over LAN.

My take as a UX Designer

Keep: The hero system and its death penalty. Reviving at the altar with level and items intact is a fair cost: strong enough to make you protect your hero, light enough that one mistake doesn’t end the match. I’d also keep the sound feedback, which lets players manage a whole base without checking every corner of the map.

Change: I don’t know the team’s constraints, which in 2002 may have included technical or screen-space limits, so I wouldn’t touch the limit. The gap I’d flag is how the limit is communicated. The first time a box selection goes over 12 units, a short hint could point the player to control groups (Ctrl + a number key). One message, one time, and the limit stops feeling like a bug and starts reading as a rule. In September 2026, Blizzard raised the maximum selection from 12 to 24 units in Warcraft III: Reforged Patch 3.0, which suggests the friction was real for many players. But raising the limit changes how the game plays, while a hint fixes the communication and leaves the design intact.

What this taught me

The clearest lesson from looking at Warcraft III as a designer: usability and engageability do different jobs. Familiar controls and good feedback made the game easy to learn, but the hero, the shorter matches and the flying units are what made me want to keep playing. And not every frustration is a problem to fix: the hero’s death was a cost worth keeping, while the selection limit only needed better communication.

If you played Warcraft III back then, did the 12-unit limit bother you too? I’d like to know.
